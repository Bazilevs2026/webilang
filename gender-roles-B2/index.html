Women’s Equality in the Soviet Union: Progress and Contradictions

Read for argument, evidence and qualification — not just facts.

In the twentieth century, the Soviet Union was unusually ambitious in promoting women’s legal equality, education, and participation in paid work. Soviet ideology treated women’s employment and economic independence as important parts of social equality. Women entered universities and professional fields such as medicine, science, teaching, and engineering on a large scale. This made the USSR more advanced than many Western countries in some areas, especially in combining higher education with mass female employment.

The legal framework was also progressive for its time. The Soviet Constitution of 1936 formally guaranteed women equal rights with men in economic, political, cultural, and social life. It also connected equality with access to work, education, maternity protection, and childcare. In practice, Soviet women participated in the labour force at very high rates and were much more economically independent than women in many other countries of the same period.

Germany offers a more complicated comparison. German women gained the right to vote and stand for election in 1918, and the Weimar Constitution of 1919 stated that men and women had, in principle, the same civil rights and duties. After the Second World War, West Germany’s Basic Law of 1949 explicitly declared that men and women have equal rights. However, legal equality did not immediately remove traditional family structures.

The Soviet model also had serious contradictions. Women’s high employment did not mean equal conditions at home. Research on Soviet society found that women earned less than men on average while spending much more time on household work. Their exceptionally high participation in paid employment therefore created a particularly heavy double burden.

The USSR can therefore be described as highly advanced in women’s education, employment, and formal equality, but not as a society that had achieved complete gender equality. Germany developed some comparable legal principles early, while both societies continued to preserve important traditional gender expectations.

GRAMMAR

Two structures for discussing gender roles

One recycled structure + one argumentation structure.

Rule 1: preposition + -ing

After a preposition, a verb normally takes -ing.

by + -ing · without + -ing · after + -ing · instead of + -ing

Women gained independence by entering higher education.

Rule 2: concession and contrast

although / even though + clause
despite / in spite of + noun or -ing
whereas / while + contrasting clause

Although both parents worked, she did more domestic work.

Important: The text below is an original study paraphrase of the ideas discussed in Justin Baldoni's talk; it is not a verbatim transcript. For exact quotations, open the official TED transcript.

In his talk, Justin Baldoni questions traditional expectations about masculinity. He asks whether boys and men are often encouraged to hide vulnerability, avoid admitting fear, and perform a narrow version of strength. These social expectations can make honest emotional communication more difficult.

The speaker argues that being courageous does not require rejecting empathy or tenderness. Instead, men can challenge stereotypes, listen seriously to women's experiences, and take responsibility for their behaviour. A more inclusive understanding of masculinity leaves room for emotional intelligence, openness, and care.

Consider the difference between the image people are expected to present and their actual feelings. An important question is how family, friends, and culture reinforce traditional norms — and how individuals can choose to change them.

SPEAKING

Culture, policy and gender roles

Use three lenses: cultural tendencies, social policy and individual experience.

Important caveat: Hofstede’s “Masculinity/Femininity” dimension (now often reframed as motivation toward achievement and success) is not a direct measure of women’s rights or of how “masculine” individual men are. It describes broad cultural tendencies around competition, achievement, care and cooperation.

1. Culture

Does a more “feminine” culture necessarily mean greater equality between men and women? Why not?

2. Soviet policy

Was Soviet equality mainly legal, economic, social — or all three? What did the “double burden” reveal?

3. Germany

How can a society establish legal equality while traditional family expectations remain strong?

4. Baldoni

Are strength and vulnerability opposites? Explain using one example from the talk.

5. Elena

Which parts of Elena’s life were made possible by social structures, and which by her own choices?

6. Synthesis

Which differences are better explained by culture, which by policy, and which by personal expectations?
